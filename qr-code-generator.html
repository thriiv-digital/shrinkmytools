About QR Codes

A QR (Quick Response) code is a two-dimensional barcode that can store URLs, text, contact details, Wi-Fi credentials, and more — readable instantly by any modern phone camera. They're used everywhere from restaurant menus and business cards to product packaging and event tickets, because they remove the friction of typing out a long link.

Choosing an error correction level

QR codes include built-in redundancy so they still scan even if partially damaged, dirty, or obscured (for example, by a logo placed in the middle). Higher error correction means the code can tolerate more damage, but produces a visually denser pattern:

  • Low — tolerates about 7% damage. Use for clean digital displays.
  • Medium — tolerates about 15% damage. Good default for most uses.
  • Quartile — tolerates about 25% damage. Good for print materials that may get worn.
  • High — tolerates about 30% damage. Best if you plan to place a logo over the center.

Tips for reliable scanning

  • Keep strong contrast between foreground and background colors — avoid similar shades.
  • Don't shrink a printed QR code below about 2cm × 2cm, or distant scanning may fail.
  • Always test your QR code with a real phone camera before printing it at scale.
  • Keep a quiet margin (blank space) around the code — most generators, including this one, include it automatically.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do QR codes expire?

No — a QR code generated here is a static, permanent encoding of whatever text or link you entered; it doesn't expire or depend on this site staying online.

How much text can a QR code hold?

Up to about 4,300 alphanumeric characters, though very long text produces a dense, harder-to-scan code — short URLs work best.

Will a colored QR code still scan correctly?

Yes, as long as there's strong contrast between the foreground and background colors — avoid similar shades, like light blue on white.
