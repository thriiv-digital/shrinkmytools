How Loan Amortization Works

Most loans and mortgages use a fixed monthly payment calculated so the loan is fully paid off by the end of the term. Early in the loan, most of each payment goes toward interest; later on, more goes toward principal — this is why paying off a mortgage feels slow at first even though the payment never changes.

What extra payments do

Adding even a small extra amount to your monthly payment goes entirely toward principal, which reduces the interest charged on all future payments — this can meaningfully shorten a loan term and reduce total interest paid, which is why the extra payment field in this calculator recalculates the full payoff timeline.

What this doesn't include

  • Property taxes and homeowners insurance (often bundled into a mortgage escrow payment)
  • Private mortgage insurance (PMI), if applicable
  • Origination fees, closing costs, or lender-specific charges
  • Variable/adjustable interest rates (this assumes a fixed rate for the full term)

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my payment slightly different from what my bank quoted?

Banks often include escrowed items like property tax and insurance in the quoted "payment," and may round differently — this calculator shows the pure principal-and-interest payment only.

Does paying extra every month really make a big difference?

Yes — because interest is calculated on the remaining balance, extra principal payments reduce every future interest charge, which compounds significantly over a long loan term.

What happens if I enter 0% interest?

The calculator switches to simple even division of the loan amount across the term, since the standard amortization formula requires a non-zero rate.
